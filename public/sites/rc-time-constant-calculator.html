In Brief

The time constant (τ) equals the product of resistance and capacitance (τ = R × C). After one time constant, the capacitor charges to 63.2% of the applied voltage; after five time constants (5τ), it is considered practically fully charged (99.3%). The cutoff frequency (fc) of an RC filter is calculated by dividing 1 by (2π × R × C), where signal power drops by 3 dB (~70.7% voltage).

Calculation Method & Governing Formulas

RC circuits represent first-order linear dynamic systems governed by ordinary differential equations. When connected to a DC step voltage or an AC sinusoidal source, the physical behavior is characterized by the following foundational relations:

Time Constant (τ)

τ = R × C

Measured in seconds (s). Quantifies how rapidly the capacitor charges or discharges through series resistance R.

Charging Capacitor Voltage

Vc(t) = V × (1 − e−t / τ)

Where V is supply voltage, t is elapsed time, and e ≈ 2.71828 is Euler's constant.

Discharging Capacitor Voltage

Vc(t) = V × e−t / τ

Exponential decay from initial voltage V down towards zero as stored electrical charge dissipates.

Filter Cutoff Frequency (fc)

fc = 1 / (2π × R × C)

The half-power (-3 dB) frequency where capacitive reactance equals resistance: Xc = R, reducing amplitude by 29.3% (output = 70.7% Vin).

Practical Design Example

Benchmark Problem: R = 10 kΩ · C = 100 μF · V = 5 V · t = 500 ms

Consider a classic RC timing block consisting of a 10 kΩ resistor and a 100 μF capacitor powered from a 5 V DC supply:

1. Time Constant (τ) 10,000 Ω × 100 μF = 1.0 s
2. Full Charge (5τ) 5 × 1.0 s = 5.0 s (99.3%)
3. Cutoff Frequency (fc) 1 / (2π × 1.0) ≈ 0.159 Hz (159 mHz)
4. Charge at 500 ms 5 × (1 − e−0.5) = 1.97 V
5. Discharge at 500 ms 5 × e−0.5 = 3.03 V

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What are the primary applications of an RC circuit?
RC circuits are fundamental building blocks in analog and digital electronics. Key practical applications include:
  • Time Delays & Oscillators: Establishing precise timing periods for reset circuits, delay lines, and timer ICs such as the 555 timer.
  • Signal Filtering: Implementing Low-Pass Filters (LPF) to suppress high-frequency noise and High-Pass Filters (HPF) to eliminate DC offsets while passing AC signals.
  • Pushbutton & Switch Debouncing: Absorbing mechanical bounce spikes when mechanical contacts open or close, preventing false multiple triggers in microcontrollers.
  • PWM Smoothing (DAC): Converting digital Pulse Width Modulated (PWM) outputs into a stable, ripple-free analog DC voltage level.
How do I debounce a pushbutton using an RC circuit?
Mechanical buttons physically bounce for 1 to 5 milliseconds upon closing. A widely adopted hardware debouncing circuit uses a 10 kΩ pull-up/pull-down resistor paired with a 100 nF ceramic capacitor.

This combination yields a time constant of:
τ = R × C = 10,000 Ω × 100 × 10−9 F = 1.0 ms.

This 1 ms time constant smooths out contact bounce without introducing noticeable lag for user interaction. For maximum signal integrity, connect the capacitor output to a Schmitt Trigger input gate (e.g., 74HC14 or microcontroller GPIO with Schmitt input enabled) to provide sharp, clean logic level transitions.
Why are five time constants (5τ) specifically considered full charge?
Capacitor charging follows an asymptotic exponential curve: Vc(t) = V × (1 − e−t/τ). Mathematically, reaching exactly 100.00% charge would require infinite time.

However, calculating the percentage at 5τ:
1 − e−5 = 1 − 0.006738 ≈ 99.33%.

Because component manufacturing tolerances typically range from ±1% to ±10%, and voltage supply variations exceed 0.7%, the remaining 0.67% of charging voltage is negligible in real-world engineering. Thus, 5τ is universally accepted across electronics engineering standards as practical full charge.
